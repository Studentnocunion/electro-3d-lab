Working Principle of Phototransistor

A phototransistor is a light-sensitive semiconductor device that works like a Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT). Instead of using an electrical base current as the main control, it uses incident light to control collector current.

1. Light Absorption

When light falls on the photosensitive region, photons generate electron-hole pairs inside the semiconductor.

2. Carrier Generation

The generated charge carriers contribute to an effective base current in the transistor.

3. Current Amplification

The transistor action amplifies the light-generated current, producing a larger collector current.

4. Light Detection

As incident light increases, collector current generally increases. This allows the device to detect light intensity.

Construction and Terminals

A phototransistor is commonly constructed using an NPN or PNP transistor structure with a light-sensitive region. Light enters through a transparent window in the package.

Collector (C)

Collects charge carriers and carries the output current through the transistor.

Base (B)

The photosensitive region generates carriers when light falls on it. Some devices have no externally accessible base terminal.

Emitter (E)

Supplies charge carriers as part of transistor operation.

Light-Receiving Window

A transparent region allows incident light to reach the photosensitive semiconductor.

Note: Many phototransistors are available with only two external terminals: Collector and Emitter. The Base may be internally unconnected.

Phototransistor Operating Modes

The operating condition of a phototransistor depends on the incident light and the external circuit.

1. Cutoff Mode

When little or no light falls on the device, the light-generated current is very small. The collector current is mainly leakage current.

2. Active Mode

When light falls on the photosensitive region, charge carriers are generated and collector current increases. This mode is commonly used for light detection.

3. Saturation Mode

Under sufficient illumination and suitable circuit conditions, the transistor can enter saturation. Increasing light further may no longer produce a proportional increase in collector current.

Important Observation

In the active region, collector current generally increases with incident light intensity. The exact response depends on the device and external circuit.

Applications of Phototransistor

1. Optical Switches

Detects the presence or absence of objects using light.

2. Object Counters

Counts moving objects on conveyor belts using light interruption.

3. IR Receivers

Detects infrared signals in electronic control systems.

4. Smoke Detectors

Detects changes in light caused by smoke particles.

5. Optocouplers

Receives light signals to provide electrical isolation between circuits.

6. Light Barriers

Detects interruptions in a light beam for security and automation systems.

Phototransistor vs Photodiode vs BJT

Phototransistor

Input: Light

Output: Amplified collector current

Working: Uses transistor action to amplify the light-generated signal.

Application: Optical switches and object detection.

Photodiode

Input: Light

Output: Photocurrent

Working: Converts incident light into electrical current.

Application: Optical communication and light sensing.

Ordinary BJT

Input: Base current or voltage

Output: Collector current

Working: Uses an electrical input signal to control collector current.

Application: Amplifiers and electronic switches.

Important Phototransistor Formulas

1. Collector Current

IC ≈ β × Iphoto-base

Collector current is approximately related to the effective light-generated base current through current gain.

2. Current Gain

β = IC / IB

Represents the ratio of collector current to base current when the device operates in its active region.

3. Power

P = VCE × IC

Estimates the power dissipated in the collector-emitter path.

4. Light Response

IC generally increases as light intensity increases.

The relationship depends on the device, operating voltage and circuit conditions.

Note: These are simplified relationships. Actual phototransistor response is device-dependent and may be nonlinear. Always check the datasheet for operating limits.

Quick Revision Notes

Definition

A phototransistor is a light-sensitive semiconductor device that converts light into an electrical signal.

Basic Structure

It is based on a transistor structure, commonly with a light-sensitive collector-base region.

Three Terminals

Collector, Base and Emitter. Some phototransistors have only two externally accessible terminals.

Working Principle

Incident light generates charge carriers, producing an effective base current and controlling collector current.

Light Sensitivity

Collector current generally increases with increasing incident light intensity.

Main Advantage

Provides internal current amplification, making it useful for detecting relatively weak light signals.

Operating Modes

Cutoff, Active and Saturation.

Applications

Optical switches, object counters, IR receivers, smoke detectors and optocouplers.

Technical Glossary

Phototransistor

A transistor that responds to incident light.

Photocurrent

Electrical current generated due to incident light.

Base Current

Current entering the base terminal of a transistor.

Collector Current

Current flowing through the collector-emitter path.

Current Gain (β)

The ratio of collector current to base current in the active region.

Cutoff Region

Operating region where collector current is very small.

Active Region

Operating region where collector current responds to the light-generated signal.

Saturation Region

Operating region where the transistor is fully switched on by the external circuit conditions.

Optical Sensor

A device that detects or measures light.

Infrared Radiation

Electromagnetic radiation commonly used in optical sensing and remote-control systems.
